The Conclusion section of the book ends with an anecdote about a conversation between neurosurgeon Jeffrey Jefferson and computer scientist Alan Turing. While discussing Turing’s experiments about teaching machines to perform certain tasks, Jefferson asked him, “But who was learning? You or the machine?” Turing answered, “Well, I suppose we both were.” This quote made me contemplate for a long time and consolidated all my thoughts about the book into one conclusion: the book believes AI alignment – and maybe AI development more broadly – is a process of evolution. Various ethical and social issues that have sprung up in recent years illustrate this process, highlighting that consultation and collaboration is necessary to evolve in the right direction. Despite the challenges and potential dangers, the author says, “Alignment will be messy. How could it be otherwise? Its story will be our story. For better or worse. How could it not?” And I agree with him.

I reflected on this book in the context of a few distinct themes, as discussed in detail below. Some of these come from similar experiences I had in other classes this semester. Some others are long-standing themes I’ve thought about since undergraduate college. I was not aware of the exact issue of AI alignment before taking this course, so the contents of this book were new to me. However, it seemed to be closely related to other issues in AI ethics, and I’ve reflected on some of them in the past. This book gave me the opportunity to revisit those opinions and make them more informed based on the perspective of alignment. As a result, I concluded that AI alignment could be the definitive issue encapsulating all AI ethics questions at this moment. Other questions, like moral questions for example, are also important frontiers. But I think alignment is able to operationalize those concerns into a technical question that can be solved in a technical way, and since AI is a technical endeavor, this formulation feels more powerful than others.

## I. The Headliners

The book, especially in initial chapters, discussed many famous cases of AI ethics issues – or rather, debacles. These included the ProPublica-COMPAS controversy. But it was preceded by context about the larger field: how data science applications in criminology pre-date COMPAS by decades and how the person who owned COMPAS started out as essentially a data scientist, much like regular techies. I read about the COMPAS case study in another class on data ethics this semester, where many people concluded that the potential harms of the technique should have been obvious to the stakeholders involved. However, this book provided much more context on the field of criminology which challenged that conclusion. It started with a story from a few decades ago, when an Illinois judge commissioned researchers from the University of Chicago and Northwestern University to explore the idea of data analysis for criminal justice. This put things into a broader perspective for me: recent discussions tend to assume that COMPAS just sprang up out of nowhere, but the book presented how the field has been developing for decades with positive reception from legal and policy professionals. That explains why COMPAS was implemented so widely in such a short time. Knowing this context helped me avoid demonizing the tech people involved. And beyond COMPAS, it pointed towards a broader issue I’ve been thinking about since undergraduate studies: on average, ethicists are quite detached from reality, activists attack without responsibility, and policy people often miss the point.

I say this as a student of policy and philosophy. I say this as someone who worked in public policy, as a social science major, as someone who was once encouraged to be skeptical of engineers and denounce much of tech as inhuman. I switched sides(?) and joined a STEM program in grad school because I thought there was merit in what AI developers were trying to do. There wasn’t just power, there was responsibility too. Because ironically, AI developers adhere to benchmarks which make their failures measurable, while some other stakeholders who criticize them have no quantifiable benchmarks for substantiating their claims. There is no doubt that the failures of COMPAS were serious and caused immeasurable harm. It is also possible that COMPAS developers were irresponsible in their practice. But when we extend that example to represent all issues about alignment or AI ethics, we should be careful not to over-generalize or use ad-hominem attacks. I refuse to believe that tech people as a community – including the billionaires – are out to destroy society. This book, and the way it described people’s struggles to make systems more aligned, is evidence that they are not. Yet I see the above narrative still being repeated in venues small and large. The fear is real, and some of it is valid, too. But this book validated my decision of becoming a data scientist because we as professionals are trying to find solutions.


## II. My Part of the World

Of course, the above is not to say that engineers are always right. Some things about how AI is done continue to bother me, and one of them is that it doesn’t work well across cultural contexts. In the other class on data ethics, I presented a case study about data labelers who work in outsourcing firms. We shared a fictional story from the film Humans In The Loop where Nehma, a tribal Indian woman working as a data labeler, feels conflicted about assigning inaccurate labels to samples simply to comply with client guidelines. In the course of her moral struggles with her job, Nehma learns that the generative models she’s helping develop don’t recognize what a tribal Indian woman looks like. When asked to generate images of people like Nehma, the models at best show Native American people and at worst, people who look nothing like Nehma. The book also spoke about this issue in the chapter on Representation. It discussed a real-life example of a facial recognition model that couldn’t detect certain races accurately. The most commonly discussed solution to such issues is getting more representative data. The book also discussed it, but it went beyond that. 

It made some interesting points there:
Models can never be completely unbiased.
Questions of representation quickly transition from empirical to philosophical: whose morals should algorithms replicate? This is easy to recognize but difficult to answer, and there are many layers to unpack.
We need to think about model progress over time instead of obsessing over singular examples: as social standards evolved, algorithms have also evolved over the past few decades — they are being developed to keep pace with changing social norms and it’s reasonable to believe that models will keep following humans with a certain lag in terms of ethics. This is a realization more people need to have. Once this is accepted as axiomatic, we can think about how to do it best.


## III. But Why and Why Me

In the context of all of this, I thought alignment raised questions about the AI-fication of everything. Given how difficult it is to get AI to be human-aligned, I kept wondering why we are doing this in the first place.

This ties into my personal journey. As I said above, I am part of the larger transition in methods from human analysis to AI: I deliberately brought ML and AI to my social science work without anyone asking me to, because I firmly believe that it was necessary to do so. My reason: scale. ML and AI was the reason huge datasets existed and could be analyzed, taking us closer to comprehensive datasets for variables of social scientific interest. But now that I am a tech person, I see AI being used – and developed – for decision making. That is not what I had envisioned, especially as a social scientist. I feel a sense of belonging in the tech community and I see the immense benefits AI-led decision making may have, but I know that in some aspects of life, you cannot rush. You cannot pursue efficiency – or even predictability, for that matter – there because that kills the entire purpose of the task. When it comes to social dynamics, you cannot exert artificial constraints. Those are complex and distributed systems, so they must evolve organically; they must arrive at their decisions naturally, because there is power (and long-term efficiency, by the way, often in the form of resilience) in their natural ways. In my view, the role of AI is to provide information and insights, not recommend decisions. We as tech people are quickly forgetting about this distinction and as an interdisciplinary person, I can say that that is what is making other disciplines so concerned about what we are doing. If alignment is a problem that can never have a complete solution, can we solve it by not seeking it and recognizing it as a bug rather than a feature?


## IV. Anything Rules

But who am I to say anything about what should be done in the field of AI? I hardly understand how anything works. And as we learned in the classroom, data and XAI methods can be used as evidence to argue both sides of an ethical issue. The book took that further: many examples in the book were ongoing debates with people arguing both sides based on the same evidence. COMPAS was a canonical example of this as well. This tied nicely into Dr Bent’s insight that XAI is for developers, it doesn’t produce any normative conclusions.

Dr Bent gave a guest lecture on XAI in the other data ethics class I took this semester. There, she said that XAI has applications for AI developers, not necessarily for end users or the general public. Explainability is about the model, it has a very specific scope: explain the model and not much beyond it. It doesn’t give any value judgements about the subject matter. The diagnoses and decisions that are made based on its results are completely the analyst’s prerogative. And according to my experience in the ML Court activity, the rationale for those decisions can sometimes be skipped entirely by just shouting loudly.
Probably my biggest takeaway from this course is this placement of XAI in the development pipeline – and in the world in general. I find it really cool that there’s an entire discipline dedicated to explaining how some methods work. To my beginner-level mind, this feels like a meta-methods discipline. The book seemed to concur: the author didn’t provide any prescriptions about how AI development should proceed henceforth, but showed how valuable it was to understand the ongoing issues properly. I have a personal value of respecting each person’s agency in determining their moral preferences. I believe humans have an innate sense of what is right and wrong, and that we can rely on that sense to make decisions – especially collective decisions. Individuals’ choices may look contrary to each other, but in the larger scheme of things, they often coexist seamlessly. For complex problems like AI alignment, I trust the humans working on it – and those who aren’t working on it but are affecting it indirectly as users, investors, policymakers, observers, citizens – to choose their individual correctness which will steer the whole effort in the correct direction.


## V. Conclusion: The Book is Optimistic and So Am I

As a result, I am optimistic, just like the book is.
